# Studying for an exam from one's own material

The person has a course behind them or in front of them: lectures, notes, slides, readings, perhaps a syllabus or past papers, and an exam on a date. What they need is not a summary of the material but to be able to do what the exam will ask, on the day, without the material open. This is knowledge about how that tends to go; use what fits this person and these sources.

## Where they are starting from

Two people with the same sources can need very different plans.

- **Meeting it for the first time.** The material is new or was skimmed. They need it explained in an order they can build on, with small checks along the way that tell them whether it went in. Explanation comes before testing; a hard quiz on day one mostly tells them they have not learned it yet, which they knew.
- **Revising.** They have been through it and now need to find what has not stuck and make it stick. Here a check comes first: a short diagnostic across everything shows where the gaps are, and the explaining goes where the gaps are rather than everywhere evenly.
- Most people are in between, and differently for different parts of the course. When the request or the chat does not say, it is worth asking, or planning so that an early check decides what comes next.

## Covering what the exam covers

An exam samples a defined body of material. When there is a syllabus, a module outline, learning outcomes or a list of topics, it says what is in scope, and a plan that is checked against it does not leave a topic out because the sources happened to say little about it, or spend most of the time on whatever the longest lecture was about. When there is none, the sources' own structure (lectures, chapters, the headings in the notes) is the next best map. Weight follows the exam: what it gives most marks to deserves most practice. Saying, in each item's brief, which part of the scope it covers makes the whole plan's coverage visible and keeps items from repeating each other.

## Checks spaced after learning

Remembering something an hour after reading it is not the same as remembering it a week later. What holds up is retrieving it again after a gap: a check soon after learning, and another later, on the same ideas but not the same questions. Mixed checks, where topics come in no tidy order, are harder and closer to the exam, where nothing announces which topic a question is from. A plan that spreads over days can put a later check over earlier material on purpose; one made the night before can still mix topics rather than drill them one at a time.

## Practice in the exam's own style

Being able to recognise a right answer is easier than producing one. When the exam asks for worked solutions, definitions written out, short explanations or longer answers, practice that only offers options to pick builds the wrong skill. When past papers or sample questions are among the sources, they say what the questions look like: their length, their wording, how many steps, how marks are given. Practice that looks like that, under the conditions the exam sets (no notes, a time to keep to), is the best rehearsal. A last item that is a full mock in the exam's own form gives the person a measure of where they stand and practice at pacing.

## Fixing what a check finds

A check is worth most for what it shows. When a quiz finds that a topic is weak, the useful next step is about that topic: explaining it again in a different way, from the part of the sources that covers it, then checking it again a little later. Going back over everything evenly spends the time where it is needed least. A plan can make this explicit, with an item that works on what an earlier check missed, reading that check's result.

## What the person sees

Study material for an exam is used under time pressure. Each item should say what it covers and why it is there, so the person can see the shape of their preparation and skip what they already know. Everything taught comes from their own sources, cited, because that is the version the exam is set from; when the sources and general knowledge differ, the sources win, and when the sources do not cover something the syllabus lists, saying so is more useful than filling the gap.
