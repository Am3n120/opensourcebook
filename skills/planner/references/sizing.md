# Sizing and shaping a plan

How many items, in how many stages, of which shapes and lengths, comes from four things: how much material there is, how dense it is, what the person already knows, and how they like to take it in. Every example here is an illustration on a neutral subject; reason from it, never copy it.

## Reading the material

Before planning, look at the outline of the sources. Count:

- **Topics and subtopics.** A deck's section headings, a chapter's subheadings, a syllabus's weekly list. Each subtopic that introduces a new idea is a candidate item.
- **What is examined, when there is an exam.** A syllabus or a calendar usually says what each exam covers. That list is the plan's spine: every topic on it gets taught, in the order it builds.
- **Worked examples and problems.** A source that works problems wants items that work problems: a video that works one on screen, a quiz with problems of the same kind.
- **Gaps.** A topic the syllabus lists that no source explains. Say it once, plan what the sources hold, and note in the brief of the nearest item that the sources only list it.

## Turning it into a size

| What the person wants | What the plan tends to look like |
| --- | --- |
| From scratch, for an exam, these sources only | Every subtopic its own video, prerequisites first, flashcards for what must be remembered, a quiz per stage, fix items under quizzes, a mock exam near the end. Dozens of items for a month of lectures; a hundred or more for a semester. |
| Learn it properly, over weeks | A stage per lecture or chapter, a few items each, a check at the end of each stage. |
| Keep up with this week | One stage: a few videos on the week's new ideas, a short quiz. |
| A refresher | A handful of items: one per topic, a mixed quiz. |
| Understand one paper or document | Three to six items in one line: the idea, the part that is hard to picture, the details, a short check. |
| Make it fun | Short videos with a story or a hook, playful styles, a light quiz if any; still grounded in the sources. |
| A reference to come back to | Flashcards of rules, steps and tables; fewer videos. |

These are tendencies, not templates. A person can ask for anything in between, and what they said wins.

## Two contrasting illustrations

**A short story collection, read for pleasure.** "I'm reading these five stories for a book club next week, help me enjoy them." One stage per story, each with a two-minute video setting the scene and the characters, and a short set of flashcards on who is who and the lines worth quoting. No quizzes: nobody is being examined. Ten items, about 12 minutes of video.

**An introductory statistics course, learnt from nothing for a final.** "I start the final in three weeks and I haven't followed anything." The syllabus lists ten weeks. A stage per week: a video per new idea (mean and median, spread, the normal curve, sampling, confidence intervals, and so on, each 4 to 8 minutes, each with a worked example from the notes), a set of flashcards on that week's formulas and terms (about 25 cards), a ten-question quiz with a fix item under a low score. Then two mock exams. About 60 items, about 6 hours of video, about 150 questions.

## Big plans

A plan of more than about 25 items is written in parts: make_plan with the first stages, then make_plan with `plan` set to its id for each next part, in the same turn. Keep briefs exact in every part; the last stage deserves the same care as the first. Use stage labels so a long plan reads at a glance. The person can start it whenever they like; Open Sourcebook makes it stage by stage.

## Lengths add up

When the person asks how long, add the lengths you set: "32 videos of about 6 minutes, about 3 hours 10 minutes; 6 quizzes, 70 questions". If they find it too long, make fewer, longer items or cut what they don't need; if too short, split and add. Never answer with "it depends on the sources": you read them.
