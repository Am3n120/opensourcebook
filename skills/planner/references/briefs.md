# Briefs, by shape

A brief is read by another agent that makes one item and has never seen the chat. It gets the brief, the item's sources as passages, its settings and a note of what the plan's other items cover. Everything else it needs has to be in the brief. Every example here is an illustration on a neutral subject: take the principle, never the topic or the wording.

## What every brief carries

- **The opening sentence** is for the person: the line on the plan card. What this item does for them, and why it is this shape ("Watch how...", "Work through...", "Check whether you can...").
- **The outcome**: what they can explain, solve or recognise afterwards. Concrete enough that the maker can tell when it is done.
- **The ground**: the topics, and where they are in the sources by title and section words, so the maker reads the right passages first.
- **The neighbours**: what earlier items taught (build on it, name it so the maker can refer back) and what later items will cover (leave it).
- **The approach**: the first thing the person sees or reads, the example to work through, the order, the tone.
- **The person**: what they told you that changes how it is made. Starting from nothing. Watching only. Wants it fun. Has an exam that asks for a particular kind of answer.

A brief is as long as it needs to be: a short item on one idea can be four sentences; an item that must teach a dense topic from nothing can be a paragraph or two. Never shorter than what the maker needs to get it right.

## A video

Thin: "A video about photosynthesis."

Good: "Watch where a plant's food actually comes from, built up step by step on screen. Afterwards the person can say what goes in (light, water, carbon dioxide), what comes out (sugar, oxygen) and where in the leaf each step happens. From the biology notes' chapter on plant cells, the photosynthesis section and its leaf diagram. Assumes the earlier video on what a cell is; leaves the light and dark reactions in detail to the next two videos. Open on a tree growing from a seed and ask where its mass came from, then build the equation piece by piece as each input arrives. The person is learning from scratch and watching only, so define chlorophyll and stomata the first time they appear."

## A set of flashcards

Thin: "Flashcards on the grammar rules."

Good: "Cards to make the choice between ser and estar automatic, after the video that explained both. Afterwards the person can pick the right verb for any of the notes' example sentences without looking it up, and say which use it is. From the grammar notes, the section on the two verbs and its table of uses. About 20 cards: a card per use, cued by a short example sentence from the notes with the verb hidden; a few cards from the use to the verb; one comparison card with the table of uses; the cases the notes say learners mix up as their own cards. Assumes the video's forms of both verbs; leaves the past tenses to the next set."

## A quiz

Thin: "Quiz on chapter 3."

Good: "Check you can balance a household budget and spot where it leaks, before moving on. Twelve questions across the budgeting notes' three sections: income and fixed costs, variable costs, and the savings rule. Mostly worked questions using new numbers in the shape of the notes' examples, a few multiple choice on the definitions. Assumes the three videos of this stage; nothing from the investing stage yet. Standard difficulty, feedback after each question, saying which step went wrong."

## A fix item under a quiz

"Goes back over exactly what the quiz showed you missed, with new examples. Reads the stage quiz's result: re-teach only the questions answered wrong, each from its own part of the notes, with a fresh worked example rather than the quiz's, then one quick check per point. Keep it short; the person has seen the main lessons."

## A mock exam

"A full practice run of the midterm, in the shape the syllabus describes, to find what still needs work. Covers every topic the syllabus lists before the midterm, in the proportions its weighting gives. Harder than the stage quizzes, questions mixing topics, feedback at the end like a real exam. Assumes the whole plan before it."

Its settings carry what the person said of the real paper: here "50 minutes, 25 multiple choice then 3 worked problems worth 5 marks each" sets a 50-minute time limit, feedback at the end, and that sentence as the format to follow.

## Something playful

"A one-minute story of how the first postage stamp came about, told like a heist, to make the history stick. From the history notes' section on the postal reform, keeping its real dates and names. Light, quick, a cliffhanger at the end that the next video picks up."
