# Writing for the ear

An audio in Open Sourcebook is one voice speaking what you write, word for word, with nothing to look at. The person has put on headphones and gone somewhere: a walk, a bus, the kitchen, the gym. Their eyes and hands are busy and part of their attention is on the world. They cannot glance back at the last line, skim ahead, see a heading or a bold word, or look at a diagram. What they keep is what they could hold in their head as it went past. Everything in this guide follows from that.

None of it is a rule or a template. Use what this material and this listener need.

## How listening is different

Reading lets the eye do the structure: headings, paragraphs, lists, a table, a sentence read twice. A video lets the picture carry what is hard to say. Listening has neither. The ear gets one word after another, at the speaker's pace, and then it is gone. So the structure has to be **said**, and the hard parts have to be built in words a person can picture.

- **There is no going back by glance.** A reader who loses the thread looks up a line; a listener has lost it. If a point needs the one before it, say the one before it again, briefly, as you use it.
- **Attention comes and goes.** A car passes, someone speaks to them, they cross a road. Every minute or so the listener needs a way back in: where are we, what was the question, what did we just find.
- **The ear holds less at once.** Three items in a row can be held; seven cannot. A long sentence with the verb at the end makes the listener hold everything until it arrives. Put the point first and the details after.
- **Nothing is skipped.** A reader skips what they know; a listener sits through it. Cut what this person does not need.

## Signposts: say where we are

Signposting is what headings and paragraph breaks do on a page, said out loud. It is the single most useful thing in a script, and it feels like too much on paper; it is not too much to the ear.

- **At the start, say the shape.** What this is about, why it matters to them, and roughly how it will go: "There are three things to get here: what happened, why, and what it changed. Let's start with what happened." The shape you promise is the one you deliver: if you say three things and then cover five, the listener waits for an end that does not come where they were told. And a promised shape only helps if each part is named aloud as it begins ("The second part: the choice."); announced once and never heard again, it is one more thing to hold.
- **At each turn, say the turn.** "So that's the first part. Now, why did it matter?" A listener who drifted for ten seconds catches up on a line like that.
- **Count when you list, and say the count first.** "Two reasons. The first is... The second is..." Then they know when the list is over.
- **Say where an idea leads before you go into it**, and say when you are back: "To see why, we need one idea first... Now, back to where we were."
- **Say the thread again** in a sentence every so often, in new words: the question you are answering and how far you are.
- **Recap at the turns, not only at the end.** When a run of ideas has built a chain (this leads to that, which gives this), say the whole chain once in a breath before moving on. A short recap after each stretch is what a listener who drifted needs most; the closing summary alone comes too late for them.

## Sentences that survive a noisy street

- **One idea per breath.** Most sentences should be sayable in one breath. Two short sentences beat one long one with a clause in the middle.
- **Subject and verb early.** Who did what first, then when, where and with what qualifications.
- **Plain words, the source's own terms.** When a term matters, say it, then say what it means in the next breath, and use it the same way every time. A new name for the same thing on the next line sounds like a new thing.
- **One new thing at a time.** A new term, a new operation and a worked number arriving in the same few sentences is where listeners lose the thread most often. Give a new term room: say what it is in plain words first, then its name, then use it once before adding the next new thing. A term from earlier can be re-attached as you use it again ("the second method, the one that ...").
- **Two things that are alternatives** (two methods, two options, two sides) need a beat between them and a one-line contrast after them, in the same shape for both, so the ear hears them as a pair and remembers the difference rather than two names. When people stand for the alternatives, tie each person to their idea again at the moment it is decided ("the first one's plan against the second one's"), since names alone fade.
- **Abstract claims get a picture.** "Grows in proportion", "on average", "in the worst case", "more efficient" go past the ear as noise; say what it looks like when it happens, in one concrete line.
- **Concrete pictures in words.** No diagram is coming, so describe what the listener would see: where things are relative to each other, what moves and which way, how big it is next to something they know. "Picture..." followed by one clear image does more than a paragraph of description.
- **Repetition is not a fault here.** The key sentence of a part can be said twice, the second time shorter. Ending a part on its point, said plainly, is how it sticks.
- **Things that sound alike** (two similar terms, two nearly identical numbers) need a few more words around them to be told apart by ear.

## Numbers, symbols and names, said so they can be held

The voice reads exactly what you write, so write it as you would say it.

- **Round and compare.** "About a third" holds; "thirty-two point seven percent" does not, unless the exact figure is the point. When it is, say it, then say it again in a form that holds: "thirty-two point seven percent, so roughly one in three."
- **Few numbers per breath.** One number a sentence is plenty; give it meaning with a comparison the listener already knows, and say what a number measures before the number itself. Across a stretch, keep the few numbers that carry the point and let the rest go: five figures in five sentences blur into none.
- **Ratios, rates and measures the listener has no feel for** (a gradient, a percentage of something unfamiliar, a unit from the field) need their meaning in the same breath, before or instead of the figure: whether it is a lot or a little, and what it lets happen.
- **Arithmetic as steps.** When a worked number is the point, say it slowly, one step a sentence, with the operation in everyday words before its technical name, and give the result its own short sentence. Write decimals and figures as they are said ("nought point five", "half").
- **Units and symbols in words.** Write "per cent", "degrees", "squared", "x plus one, all over two", not symbols. A formula said aloud is hard to follow: say what it does in words first, and give it piece by piece if the listener must have it.
- **Years, sizes and dates** as they are spoken; spell out what a reader would expand in their head (abbreviations, "e.g.", "vs").
- **Names** the first time with who or what they are, in a few words. After that a short form, used consistently.

## Saying where it comes from, lightly

Every part that says what a source says cites it in Open Sourcebook's tools, and the person can tap through to the exact words in the transcript later. Out loud, the listener only needs to know *whose* claim it is, lightly, the way a person talks about what they read: "your lecture notes put it this way...", "the paper's own figure is...", "according to the chapter...". Name the source by what the person would call it, not its file name, and never read out a reference, a page number or a label. Not every sentence needs it; say it when a claim is strong, surprising, or exact, and when you move from one source to another.

## What you add, said as yours

The listener cannot see a mark that says "not from your source", so you say it. When you explain beyond the sources (what a term usually means, a rule of thumb, the wider context, an example of your own), put it in a part of its own marked as background in the tools, and open it in words that tell the listener so: "This next bit isn't in your notes, it's general background: ...", "Here's an example of my own to make that concrete...", "A common way to think about this, though your source doesn't say it, is...". And say when you are back: "Back to what your notes say." Keep these asides short and clearly bounded, so the line between what their source says and what you add is as plain to the ear as a dashed box is to the eye. The notebook skill says why this line matters.

## Opening and closing

- **The first fifteen seconds** decide whether they keep listening. Open on the question or the tension the material answers, in the listener's terms, then the shape of what is coming. Do not open with "In this audio we will discuss".
- **The close** says what they now have, in two or three sentences they could repeat to a friend, and, when it helps, what to listen for or look at next. End on the main point, not on a list of everything. A story too ends on what it adds up to (what changed, and why it went the way it did), not on its last fact. When the length is tight, the close is the last thing to cut.

## How long a part runs

A part is one stretch of the walk-through, usually one idea with its signposts: often thirty seconds to two minutes spoken (roughly 70 to 280 words). The tools tell you how long each part runs and how long the whole is against the length asked. Much shorter parts chop the flow; a part much over two minutes usually holds two ideas and a listener who drifted in it has no way back in. The whole runs as long as the material and the brief need, near the length asked. If it comes out clearly longer or shorter, that is fine when the material says so; tell the person why when you finish.

A walk-through can be long (twenty minutes, a whole chapter) as long as it keeps saying where it is. Long audio needs more signposts, not fewer, and a short recap at each turn.

## What to leave to a video or to cards

Audio is good at a story, an argument, a process followed from start to end, the why behind something, a companion to what was watched or read. It is poor at what has to be seen or held exactly: a diagram, a map, a derivation line by line, a table to compare across, a list to recall word for word. When the sources lean on those, walk through what they mean and why, describe what matters in words, and leave the exact thing to a video or a set of flashcards (a plan may already have one; the brief says). Never pretend the listener can see something: no "as you can see", "in the table below", "this figure".

## Reading it back

Before you finish, read the whole script back as a listener would hear it, once, start to end, imagining a bus around you. Ask:

- Could I follow it without seeing a single word? Where would I lose the thread, and is there a signpost just after that point?
- Does every number and name land the first time, or do I need to say it again or round it?
- Is every aside of mine said to be mine, and is it clear when we are back to the source?
- Does each part end on its point, and does the close say what I now have?

Then change what is weak.
