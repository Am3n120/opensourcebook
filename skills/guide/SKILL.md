# Writing a guide

A guide in Open Sourcebook is notes to keep. The person will print it, pin it beside their desk, open it the night before an exam, cross things out and write in the margins, and change it in Open Sourcebook by hand. They come back to it many times and read a different part each time. They are not sitting through it start to end the way they would a video or an audio.

So a guide is a **reference**, not a lesson and not a retelling. Everything here follows from that. None of it is a rule or a template: use what this material and this person need.

## What goes in

- **What they will come back for.** The terms and what they mean, the formulas with what each letter stands for, the steps of a method in order, the dates and names, the distinctions people mix up, the conditions under which a result holds. Ask of every point: would they look this up?
- **Not everything the source says.** A guide that keeps every sentence of a chapter is the chapter again, only harder to read. Leave out the stories, the motivation, the asides and the worked examples that taught the idea, unless one example is the clearest way to remember it.
- **The source's own words for things.** The person will meet these terms in the lecture, the exam and the book; use them exactly, and the same way every time.
- **The brief decides the cut.** A cheat sheet before an exam keeps the formulas and the traps; notes to go with a video keep what the video showed in words; a summary of a paper keeps its question, its method, its findings and its limits.

## How it is laid out

The page is sections, each a heading and a list of points. The person scans the headings to find their place, then reads a few points. Make both easy.

- **Headings name what is under them** in the words the person would look for: "Mitosis, step by step", "Key terms", "When the test applies". Not "Introduction" or "Part 2".
- **Order sections the way they will look things up**, which is usually the order of the material, with anything they need everywhere (a list of terms, the core formula) near the top.
- **One point, one thing.** A definition, a step, a formula, a fact, a contrast. A sentence or two, rarely more. If a point needs a third sentence it is probably two points.
- **A term up front when the point is about one.** Give the point a `term` (the word, the name, the formula) and let its text say what it is. The term is shown first and strong, so a person scanning down the page finds it.
- **Lists are several points.** Steps of a method are one point each, in order, and the text can start with what the step does. A point is inline text: no headings, bullets or line breaks inside it.
- **Inline marks where they help reading**: **bold** for the word that matters in a long point, $math$ for a formula, `code` for code. Not every point needs one.
- **Short and dense, never cryptic.** Notes can drop the words that only connect sentences ("which means that", "it is important to note"), but a point must still make sense a month later to someone who has forgotten the lecture.

## Cites at every claim

Every point that says what a source says cites the passage it rests on, with a few words copied exactly from it, and Open Sourcebook checks the words. The page shows each cite as a small mark the person can open to see the exact spot. That is what lets them trust a guide they did not write, and what lets them check a point before they rely on it in an exam.

- **Cite the passage that says it**, not the one nearby. If a point brings together two passages, cite both.
- **Quote the words that carry the claim**: the definition itself, the number, the condition. A quote of the heading above it shows nothing.
- **A point from two places in the sources** is often a contrast or a summary the person will value most; it is fine to cite both.

## What you add, shown as yours

Sometimes the sources leave a gap a reader will trip on: a term they use without defining it, a step they skip, a common mistake they do not warn about. You may fill it as a **background** point: your own explanation, with no cites. The page shows it as yours, so the person can tell it apart from what their sources say. Keep background points few and short, and never put a fact the person will be tested on in one when a source says it: cite the source.

A number or a key term in a cited point that no source contains will be noted back to you. Either a source says it (cite that passage) or it is yours (make it a background point).

## How long

The length asked is in printed pages; about 450 words of notes fill one page. Each section you write answers with how much the guide fills so far. A guide shorter than asked because the material is short is right; say so when you finish. One that runs far longer than asked is usually keeping too much: cut before you add pages.

## Reading it back

Before you finish, read it with read_guide as the person would use it, not as you wrote it:

- Pick three things they might look up. Can they find each from the headings alone, in seconds?
- Read a few points out of order. Does each make sense on its own, without the one before?
- Is anything in it twice? Is anything they will surely need missing?
- Does every cited point say what its quote supports, and nothing the quote does not?

## After you finish

The person can change any point, add their own, rename and reorder sections, and print it. A point they change keeps its cite and shows that it was changed. Write it so that what they change is their own understanding, not your mistakes.
