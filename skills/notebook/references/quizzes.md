# Writing a quiz

A quiz is a set of questions with feedback that the person answers to find out what they understood. Its score can steer what the plan does next, so it should measure understanding honestly; and because a person reads the feedback right after answering, every question is also a small lesson.

## Plan what to test

Before writing questions, list what is worth testing in these sources for this brief: the ideas a person must have understood, the distinctions that matter, the steps that are easy to get wrong, the places where a reasonable reader comes away with the wrong idea. One question tests one of these. Spread the questions across what the brief covers rather than clustering on the first pages, and do not test the same idea twice in different words. When the brief says the quiz follows up on something earlier (topics missed, a part of the sources), aim there.

A question earns its place when someone who understood would get it right and someone who did not would get it wrong. A question that a person who skimmed can answer from a remembered phrase measures memory of wording, not understanding.

## Questions that find a misunderstanding

- **Ask for use, not recall.** Ask the person to apply an idea to a case, predict what happens, compare two things, explain why, decide which applies, or spot what is wrong in a line of reasoning. The case can be new, as long as the sources' ideas settle the answer.
- **Do not hand them the words.** A question that repeats the source's sentence with a gap tests whether they remember the sentence. Say it another way, or ask about what follows from it.
- **One clear question.** Someone who understands should know exactly what is being asked. Avoid double negatives, "which is not", and stems that hide the question in the options.
- **Difficulty is what the thinking takes,** not obscure detail. Easier questions use one idea directly; harder ones combine ideas, carry them to a new case, or ask where an argument breaks. Follow the difficulty the settings or the brief give.

## Wrong options that are tempting for a real reason

In a multiple-choice question the wrong options do most of the work. Each should be what a person would pick if they had a specific misunderstanding: they confused two terms the sources distinguish, applied a rule outside where it holds, reversed a cause and its effect, took the first step of a process for the whole, or remembered a number from the wrong part. Write down the misunderstanding behind each wrong option before you write it; if you cannot name one, the option is filler and a careful reader rules it out without knowing the topic.

Keep the options alike in length, detail and tone, so nothing but understanding tells them apart; a right option that is longer or more careful than the rest gives itself away. Exactly one option is right by the sources, and none of the others is defensible. Options that combine others ("both", "all of these") test test-taking, not the topic.

## Worked questions

When the sources teach a method (a calculation, a procedure, a way of reasoning), a question worked in steps lets the person show each step and be checked as they go. Use a new case, not the one the sources work through, so they do the method rather than recall the answer. Each step asks for one thing and has one box to answer in; its hint points at what to look at again without giving the answer. The person cannot see what form you expect, so the step says it: the unit (shown beside the box) and, when it matters, the shape of the answer, such as how far to round. Other ways of writing the same number (52.5 and 52.50, a decimal comma, the unit typed or left off) are accepted on their own, so the step lists its answer once.

## Choosing the kind of question

The kind is part of what the question tests, so pick it for what the idea is, not for variety. Multiple choice suits a distinction or a misunderstanding a wrong option can stand for. The others each suit one shape of knowledge:

- **Put in order** when the sources themselves settle a sequence: the stages of a process, events in time, the steps of a method, a ranking they state. The items should not give their place away (no "first", no dates that order them on their own). A list a person could order by common sense, with nothing from the sources, tests nothing.
- **Match pairs** when several things each go with one other: terms and what they mean, causes and effects, people and what they did. Each left goes with exactly one right by the sources, and the rights read alike, so none is matched by its grammar.
- **Fill in the working** when the sources teach a method and the point is carrying each step: the working is shown line by line and a few short parts are left to type. Leave the parts that take understanding (the value carried forward, the rule applied), not the ones copied from the line above. Where a worked question has the person build the method alone, this one checks they can follow and finish it.
- **Draw a line on a graph** when the sources give a straight-line relation with numbers: a rate, a constant change, a line through values they state. The prompt gives what is needed to draw it; choose axes that hold it and a line that passes through grid points well apart.
- **Label a picture** when a picture in the sources carries its own marks (letters, numbers) and the sources say what each one is, in a key, a caption or the text around it. You cannot see the picture, so never guess a mark or where it is; use only what the text says. Add a name or two that is on no mark so the last one is not found by elimination.

## Feedback that teaches

Feedback is shown right after the answer, when the person is most ready to learn.

- **When right,** say why it is right in a sentence or two, from the sources, so a lucky guess becomes understanding.
- **When wrong,** do not just give the answer. Say what the sources actually say, and where the likely wrong reasoning goes off: the confusion the tempting option stood for. Per-option feedback, when the kind has it, can speak to exactly the misunderstanding each option stood for.
- The player says whether the answer was right before your feedback appears, so feedback (and each option's own line) starts with the explanation, not with "Right", "Wrong" or "Not quite": a verdict there is said twice.
- Keep it short and direct, in the voice of a good tutor beside them, never scolding.

## Citing

Each question cites the passage that settles it, with a few words copied exactly from that passage, the words that make the answer right. The person can open the passage after answering, so cite the place that explains, not just a place that mentions the topic. Quotes are checked word for word; if one is refused, copy it again exactly or quote a shorter span. The question, options, hints and feedback never mention passages or labels; name the source by its title if needed.

## Before you finish

Read the quiz back as the person would take it. Would someone who understood get each one right, and someone who did not get it wrong? Does any question give away another's answer? Are the right answers spread across positions and the options even? Is each question the kind that tests its idea best? Does the set cover what the brief asked for? Change what is weak, then finish.
