# Writing a set of flashcards

A set of flashcards is a run of cards. Each card is one phone screen and does one job: the person reads the front, tries to answer from memory, turns the card over, and marks it **got it** or **not yet** (a card that asks a question is marked by the answer). Cards they miss come back a few cards later in the same sitting, and every card comes back on later days at growing gaps, until it sticks. So the set is not read once; it is met again and again. Write every card for the tenth time it is seen as much as for the first.

What the set is for is being able to recall: the terms, facts, steps, formulas and distinctions the brief is about, without looking. Understanding them the first time is usually another item's job (a video, a reading, the person's own class); the cards make it stay. When the brief says the person has not met the material at all, a few cards that explain may come first, but they serve the recall cards after them.

## One card, one idea

A card asks for one thing. If the answer has an "and", it is probably two cards; if the person could get half of it right, they cannot mark it honestly, and a card that cannot be marked honestly teaches nothing. Split a list of five causes into the cards that make each one recallable, or into one card whose back is the list when the list itself is what matters (then the front says how many: "the four stages of ...").

The exception is the card whose job is the whole: a comparison of three things along the same lines, the steps of a process in order, a formula and what its parts mean. Those are one card with a table, numbered steps or the formula on the back, because the person needs them together; ten small cards would lose the structure that is the point. Use them when the structure is what must be remembered, and pair them with a few single-idea cards on the parts that are easy to mix up.

## A front that makes them retrieve

The front is what does the work. A good front makes the person reach for the answer, not recognise it:

- **Ask, do not show.** "What does X do?" makes them retrieve; a front that states X and asks "true?" lets them recognise.
- **Cue the idea, not the sentence.** A front copied from the source with one word missing is often answered by the shape of the sentence. Ask from another side: the term from its meaning, the meaning from the term, the case from the rule, the rule from a case.
- **Specific enough to have one answer.** "Tell me about X" has no answer to check against. "Why does X happen when Y?" does.
- **No hints on the front** that give the answer away: no answer in the heading, no cite whose quote is the answer (put the cite on the back, where it shows after turning over).
- **Both directions when both matter.** A term the person must produce and also recognise from its definition can be two cards, one each way; most need only one.

## A back they can check against

The back is short enough to compare against at a glance: the answer first, in a few words, then, if it helps, one line on why or the one thing it is easily confused with. If the back needs a paragraph, the front asked too much. A back that holds a table, steps or a formula is fine when that is the answer; keep everything else off it.

## The kinds of card

The write tool lists the kinds of card it takes, each in its own words. Choose the kind by what the person must be able to do:

- produce an answer from a cue: a front-and-back card;
- recall the key part of a statement in context: a sentence with that part hidden (one or two gaps, each something worth recalling, never a filler word);
- tell things apart or line them up: a comparison, the table on the back;
- carry out a process: steps, in order on the back;
- use a formula: the formula on the back with what each part means;
- apply a method: a worked example, a problem on the front and the working on the back;
- name what a picture shows, when the source has a picture worth knowing: the picture on the front;
- name the parts of a picture whose own marks (A, B, 1, 2) the source's text explains, a diagram with a key: a question that checks itself, labelling the picture. You cannot see the picture, so use it only when the caption, key or passage says what each mark is;
- draw a straight-line relation the source gives with numbers (a rate, a line through values it states): a question that checks itself, drawing the line on a grid;
- check themselves on a case, with feedback: a question that checks itself (pick, fill in, match, order, label a picture, draw a line), with an explanation that says why;
- understand a point the other cards need: a card that explains. Use few, and give each one point, not a page.

Mix kinds where the material calls for it; a set of only one kind is fine when the material is of one shape (a vocabulary list is front-and-back cards). The item's settings may allow only some kinds; then use those.

## How many cards, and in what order

As many as the sources hold things worth remembering for this brief, and no more. A short note of definitions might hold eight; a chapter, thirty or forty; a course, hundreds over many sets. Do not make a card for every sentence: a source restates, gives examples and connects, and those are not each something to recall. Do not leave out what the brief says the person must know because it is hard to put on a card; find the kind that holds it.

Order the cards so they build: the terms other cards use come before them, parts before the whole that combines them, a rule before the cases that apply it. Group cards that belong together under one `section`, shown small above each card. A card may assume what earlier cards asked; it should never assume what a later card will ask.

## Words on a card

- Plain words, short sentences, the source's own terms. Bold the term the card is about when it helps the eye.
- A heading is rarely needed; the front's question names the card's point. The section's name is already shown.
- Inline marks in body text: **bold**, *italic*, `code`, $math$, used sparingly. Headings and labels are plain text.
- State the idea itself, never "the text says that ...". Never mention passages, labels, tools or how the set was made.
- An example of your own is said to be one ("say a ..."), so it is not taken for the source's data. What you add from general knowledge goes in a background block.

## Citing

Every card that says something from the sources cites the passage it comes from, with a short quote copied exactly from it that carries the claim, and the index of the block that holds the claim, counted over the front and then the back. The answer is usually the claim, so the cite usually sits on the back, where the person sees it after turning the card over and can tap through to the exact words. A card that only frames (an explain card that sets up a question) may cite nothing. Quotes are checked word for word; if one is refused, copy the words again exactly as they stand in the passage, or quote a shorter span.

## Before you finish

Read the set back as the person will meet it, card by card, and for each card ask: could I answer this from the front alone, and would I know for sure whether I got it? Is it one idea? Is the answer on the back short enough to check? Does a card give away another's answer? Is anything the brief needs missing, or anything here that is not worth remembering? Change what is weak, then finish.
