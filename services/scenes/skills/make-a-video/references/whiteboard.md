# A live whiteboard

Some explanations are best as a board that fills while the narrator talks, the way a good teacher fills a whiteboard: the viewer watches the thinking happen, and at the end sees how the pieces fit. The scene language's `board` does this (a canvas bigger than the frame, written over keep states, the camera following the writing). What makes such a board good:

- **One idea per area.** An area of the board holds one idea; the camera moves to a new area when the idea changes, often every 6 to 12 seconds.
- **Grow from what it relates to.** A side note sits beside its idea, a result under the work that made it, a consequence to the right of its cause. Not a fixed right-left-right pattern.
- **Vary the size.** A side note is small, the heart of the idea is large.
- **Pull back for connections.** A wide view (`camera BOARD`) that shows how two areas connect is a real moment; used everywhere it is noise. The last view of the whole board is usually worth it.
- **Real work, written out.** When the narration calculates, every step appears on the board in step with the words and ends in its result. For math, logic or code, show the actual object worked through, not an everyday stand-in.
- **Pictures that explain.** A drawing on the board shows the thing happening (a cutaway, a before and after, something mid-action), not a mood. A pine tree for "winter" decorates; a planet tilted away from its sun explains. If a drawing would teach nothing, write the words.
- **Short notes.** A label of one to four words is the point, not a sentence of the narration.
- **Items land on their words,** a new one every few seconds.

See the example `letter-board` for the form.
