# What viewers notice

Finished videos were watched by people who did not know their topics, on a phone held upright, and asked what they learned, where they got lost and what looked unfinished. What follows is what they said again and again, across subjects and styles. It is what a viewer tends to notice, for you to weigh; none of it is a rule.

- **The empty lower half.** The most common remark, in every style: the picture sits in the top half and the bottom of the frame stays bare for many seconds. It happens most when a beat's first state is small and its later states are given room below it from the start. A subject that is given its height, a picture of the thing itself under an abstract diagram, or the next part of the idea placed below, all answered it.
- **One thing at a time at the key step.** Where the whole explanation turns on one step, viewers asked for that step to be shown as a move they can follow (one part moving to where it goes, one case rather than several at once) and for it to stay on screen longest. Dense frames at that moment were the most named place to get lost.
- **The thing itself.** An abstract diagram for a minute and a half was followed, but viewers asked to see the real thing once: the person wearing it, the machine, the place. The concrete picture anchors the abstract one.
- **Said but not shown.** When the voice names something the picture does not have, viewers noticed the gap at once.
- **Placeholders.** A generic stand-in held under specific words (a generic shape where a specific thing was named, an empty box before its contents) reads as unfinished.
- **A new word on screen for two seconds.** A term the viewer is meant to keep needs its time, and is either used again or left unsaid.
- **Numbers that must add up.** Viewers check sums: shown parts that do not make the shown total are taken as a wrong fact.
- **The ending.** A silent hold of text after the last word felt abrupt; a closing beat that says the idea in a line and shows it, with the voice, ended it.
- **What they liked.** A consistent look that is a real design rather than boxes of words, an idea built step by step, and one moment where the picture made the idea click.
