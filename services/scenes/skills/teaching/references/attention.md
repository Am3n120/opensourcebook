# What research knows about watching

Findings to consult when deciding what moves, when, and how much is on screen. Knowledge, not rules.

**Attention**
- A thing that starts to move, appears or changes pulls the eye for about a second; ongoing motion pulls far less (Abrams and Christ 2003). Right after a cut, motion leads where eyes go (Carmi and Itti 2006; Smith and Mital 2013).
- Size, contrast and nearness to the center also pull. Words and pictures draw more looks than plain shapes.
- A cue that points at what is needed next (a highlight, a pointer, dimming the rest) helps learning, about d = 0.4 to 0.5 (Schneider et al. 2018). A cue on the wrong thing costs.

**Load**
- People hold about 3 to 4 objects in visual working memory (Luck and Vogel 1997).
- Changes during a cut, a camera move or another strong change are often missed (Rensink et al. 1997).
- Decoration that does not carry the idea costs learning (Rey 2012).
- Things that move together read as one; a stream of look-alike marks reads as one stream.

**Timing to the voice**
- The eye reaches a named thing about 200 to 400 ms after its word starts (Allopenna et al. 1998).
- A picture may lead its words a little; it should not trail them (contiguity, about d = 0.85, Ginns 2006).
- On-screen text that repeats the narration word for word hurts learning; short labels help (Adesope and Nesbit 2012).
- Timed text is read at about 12 to 17 characters a second; slower for children and second-language viewers.

**Animation and pacing**
- Animation helps when the change shown is the change in the idea, and slow enough to see (Tversky et al. 2002; Höffler and Leutner 2007). Segmenting into pieces with pauses helps (Rey et al. 2019).
- A scene boundary clears working memory (Zacks et al. 2010); keep what must be compared on one side of a cut.
- A question asked before the answer helps people remember the answer (St. Hilaire et al. 2023). Withholding what the viewer waits for builds suspense.
- Guidance that helps newcomers bores or slows experts (Kalyuga et al. 2003): who the viewer is changes how much to show.

**Motion craft**
- Stage changes: exits, then moves, then entrances (Heer and Robertson 2007). Keep identity: a thing that changes should morph or travel, not vanish and reappear.
- Interface motion usually takes 0.2 to 0.5 s, longer for longer distances and larger things; under 0.1 s reads as a jump, over 1 s as slow. Ease out on arrival, ease in on exit.

**Physics for demonstrations**
- A free fall over a height h takes sqrt(2h / 9.8) s: 0.25 s over 30 cm, 0.45 s over 1 m, 1.4 s over 10 m.
- Events under about half a second are seen but cannot be inspected. Real speed keeps them believable; a replay at a quarter to a third of real speed lets the eye compare.
- A process that takes minutes or days is shown sped up; say so.

**Reading on a phone**
- Size is what reaches the eye: a phone frame is about 65 mm wide at 350 to 400 mm. Text read at a glance needs about 27 arcminutes of cap height; read on purpose, about 18 (Legge 2007). Contrast at least 3:1 for large text, 4.5:1 for body text (WCAG 2).
- Fewer type sizes per frame read calmer; one size step between subject and support is often enough.
- Many feed viewers start with the sound off, so the burned-in captions carry the words; nothing important belongs in the caption band.
